## A Boundary Statement

This document observes a boundary that the series has established and will
maintain throughout.

Crime cannot be fixed. It is not a policy failure, an institutional design
flaw, or a correctable structural condition. Poverty, lack of education,
childhood trauma — none of these are valid diagnostic anchors for civic
infrastructure purposes, because none of them are institutions with
identifiable actors, legal frameworks that can be read and cited, and
pre-determined outcomes produced by structural mechanisms rather than human
nature.

Civic Infrastructure does not build diagnostic surfaces for conditions that
have no institutional cause. It builds them for institutions.

Civil Asset Forfeiture is not a document about crime. It is a document about
an institution — the legal mechanism that allows the government to seize
property, the financial structure that incentivizes that seizure, and the
procedural design that makes recovery structurally improbable. These are
identifiable, documentable, and addressable at the preparation level.

This document is about preparation. It is not about fixing the storm.

---

## Why This Document Exists in Civic Infrastructure

Civil Asset Forfeiture inverts the foundational legal presumption of the
Anglo-American legal tradition.

You are presumed innocent until proven guilty. Your property is not.

That is the complete structural diagnosis. Everything that follows is
elaboration, documentation, and preparation guidance.

The institution is not broken. It functions exactly as designed. The design
is the diagnostic subject.

---

## The Storm

A Category X hurricane does not care about your opinion of it. It does not
respond to outrage, to reform proposals pending in the legislature, or to the
constitutional arguments in the dissents. It is going to make landfall. The
only relevant question is what you did before it arrived.

Civil Asset Forfeiture is that storm for anyone who transports cash, operates
a cash business, owns property that others use, or lives in proximity to
activity that a law enforcement agency characterizes as suspicious — whether
or not they are charged with a crime, whether or not they are arrested, and
whether or not they are guilty of anything.

Understanding it as a storm rather than as an injustice produces the only
useful orientation: preparation before landfall.

---

## The Mechanism

Civil Asset Forfeiture is the permanent confiscation of private property by
a government agency on the basis that the property is suspected of involvement
in illegal activity.

The case is not brought against the owner. It is brought against the property.
The legal styling reflects this precisely: *State v. $40,000 in U.S. Currency*.
*United States v. One 2019 Ford F-150*. The property is the defendant. The
owner is a claimant — attempting to recover what was taken by proving, in a
civil proceeding, that their property is innocent.

The burden of proof is inverted. In a criminal proceeding, the government must
prove the owner committed a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. In a civil
forfeiture proceeding, the owner must prove their property is not connected to
illegal activity. The constitutional protections that apply to criminal cases —
the right to appointed counsel, the higher standard of proof, the
presumption of innocence — do not apply in full force to civil proceedings.

In Illinois, under the Drug Asset Forfeiture Procedure Act and related statutes,
a person need never be convicted of any crime — or even arrested, or charged —
in order to be permanently deprived of cash, a vehicle, or real property.

Once the property is seized, the owner has 45 days to file a verified claim.
If they fail to file within 45 days, the government obtains a default judgment
and the property is permanently forfeited. The 45-day clock runs whether or not
the owner knew the proceeding had been initiated. The case is in rem — brought
against the property itself — and in some circumstances the owner may not be
formally notified that it is happening.

## The Financial Incentive Structure

The agency that seizes the property benefits from the seizure.

In Illinois and in most jurisdictions, the proceeds from civil asset forfeiture
flow back to the seizing agency's operating budget. In some Illinois contexts,
law enforcement agencies receive the majority of forfeiture proceeds directly.

The federal Equitable Sharing Program extends this incentive nationally. Under
this program, a state or local law enforcement agency that seizes property
locally can transfer the forfeiture to federal prosecutors under federal law
and receive back up to 80 percent of the proceeds — regardless of what state
law would otherwise require. This mechanism allows agencies in states that
have enacted forfeiture reform legislation to bypass those reforms entirely by
routing the forfeiture through the federal program.

The financial consequence is direct and documented: many police departments
receive up to 20 percent of their total annual operating budget from civil
asset forfeiture proceeds. This creates an institutional incentive that is
structurally indistinguishable from the HOA attorney's incentive to litigate
and the school district's incentive to expand — the agency that seizes more
receives more, and the agency that decides what to seize is the same agency
that receives the proceeds.

From 2000 to 2019, 84 percent of all forfeitures executed by Department of
Justice agencies were civil forfeitures — not criminal. Among Treasury agencies,
98 percent were civil. In 2014, federal asset forfeiture receipts alone totaled
approximately $3.9 billion.

Data from 21 states show that half of all currency forfeitures involved amounts
worth less than $1,300. The median seized amount is not the proceeds of a
criminal enterprise. It is an amount small enough that the cost of hiring an
attorney to contest the forfeiture exceeds the value of what was taken.

This arithmetic is not accidental. It is the design.

## What the Storm Actually Takes

Property can be replaced. This is the first and most important preparation
posture, and it is correct as far as it goes.

If you are stopped and your car is seized, the car is a car. If you are
stopped and your jewelry is seized, jewelry can be replaced. Most people are
financially over-extended on the things most likely to be seized — vehicles,
consumer goods, cash held in the form of things purchased on credit. The
rational posture toward a car-jacking — with a baby in the back seat — is not
to fight. Give them the car. The baby is irreplaceable. The car is not.

But Civil Asset Forfeiture takes three things that are not straightforwardly
replaceable.

**Time.** Contesting a civil forfeiture proceeding, even when the owner
ultimately prevails, consumes months or years. The legal process moves at its
own pace. There is no insurance policy for time. There is no replacement for
the months spent filing claims, attending hearings, and navigating a proceeding
that the institution controls the timeline of.

**The presumption.** Once property is seized under civil forfeiture, the owner
has been treated as connected to illegal activity — not charged, not convicted,
not even arrested in many cases — and that treatment is on the record.
Subsequent interactions with institutions that run background or asset checks
may surface the seizure regardless of outcome.

**Operating cash on a specific timeline.** This is the category the storm is
most precisely calibrated to destroy.

A small business owner carrying $8,000 in cash to make Friday payroll. An
immigrant family transporting $12,000 in savings to close on a house purchase
because they do not trust banks. A market vendor carrying the weekend's
receipts. A contractor carrying cash to pay subcontractors.

These are not the proceeds of criminal enterprises. They are the operating
reality of cash-economy participants — people for whom the seized amount is not
replaceable on any timeline that does not destroy what it was meant to build.
There is no insurance policy for cash in transit. There is no line of credit
available on Friday afternoon when payroll is due and the cash is in an
evidence locker. The business that needed the money by Friday does not survive
until the court date that may be scheduled six months later.

The storm is calibrated to extract from people for whom the seized asset is
irreplaceable in practice — not in theory, not philosophically — on the
specific timeline of their specific life. These are exactly the people who will
make the rational decision not to fight, because the cost of fighting exceeds
the value of what was taken. The institution is designed around this
calculation.

## The Equitable Sharing Bypass: When State Reform Doesn't Apply

Illinois has enacted some civil asset forfeiture reforms. House Bill 303, signed
into law in 2017, improved procedural protections in certain categories — 
requiring the state to provide notice, raising minimum seizure thresholds for
currency in some drug possession contexts, and mandating data reporting to the
Illinois State Police.

These reforms do not apply when the seizing agency routes the forfeiture through
the federal Equitable Sharing Program.

By partnering with federal prosecutors and transferring the seized property into
the federal forfeiture system, a state or local agency converts a state
forfeiture — subject to Illinois procedural protections — into a federal
forfeiture, subject only to federal rules. The agency then receives back up to
80 percent of the proceeds under the equitable sharing arrangement.

The reform exists on paper. The bypass exists in practice. The agency chooses
which path to use. The owner does not choose.

## Preparation: The Bear Spray

The storm makes landfall whether or not you prepared for it. Preparation does
not prevent the storm. It changes the set of outcomes available to you when
the storm arrives.

The following is specific and practical. It is not legal advice. It is terrain
knowledge.

**Cash in transit requires a paper trail.**

If you are transporting cash — from a business to a bank, from a sale, from
savings, for a purchase — the documentation that makes that cash legible to
a law enforcement officer is the first thing that changes the encounter.

Carry on your person:
- Bank deposit slips or withdrawal records showing the cash's origin
- Business receipts, invoices, or sales records corresponding to the amount
- Prior deposit history showing regular cash handling consistent with the amount
- Any purchase agreement, contract, or written arrangement the cash is
  intended to fund
- Identification of the destination — bank name, address, contact

The cash goes in a locked container, not loose in a bag or a pocket. The
paperwork goes in your hand, not in the trunk. The first thing the officer
reads is the paper trail that makes the cash look like what it is — because
the institution is calibrated to characterize cash as suspicious, and
documentation is the counter-characterization available before any proceeding
begins.

**Know the 45-day clock.**

If property is seized, the clock to file a verified claim in Illinois begins
immediately. Missing the 45-day window results in automatic permanent
forfeiture by default judgment. The first action after any seizure is to
determine the applicable deadline and meet it — before evaluating whether the
cost of contesting is worth the value of the property.

**Photograph and document everything at the scene.**

The condition of the property, the officers present, the stated reason for
the seizure, the inventory of what was taken — documented at the moment of
seizure — is the evidentiary foundation of any subsequent proceeding. A
phone camera and a factual written account prepared immediately after the
encounter are the beginning of the diagnostic record.

**Understand the equitable sharing risk.**

If property is seized in Illinois and the seizing agency routes the forfeiture
federally, the state procedural protections that would otherwise apply do not.
This is a factor in evaluating the contesting decision and in the paper trail
preparation described above — federal forfeiture proceedings have their own
timeline, venue, and procedural requirements.

**Property that regularly transports or stores cash should be documented
accordingly.**

A vehicle used regularly for business cash transport should have that use
documented — mileage logs, business records, regular deposit patterns. Real
property associated with cash-intensive business activity should have the
business relationship documented at the property record level. Documentation
created before a seizure is categorically different from documentation created
after one.

## What Civic Infrastructure Does With This

The Diagnostic Surface for Civil Asset Forfeiture is built on the public
record that already exists and is not being assembled in one place.

Illinois State Police seizure data is publicly available through FOIA requests
but is not published in a form that allows pattern analysis — which agencies
seize most, which asset types are seized most frequently, which outcomes
result from contested versus uncontested forfeitures, which cases are routed
through equitable sharing versus state proceedings.

That data, assembled, qualified by affected-status standing — property owner,
cash-business operator, vehicle owner in a seizure-active jurisdiction —
evidence-disciplined, and published to a durable surface, constitutes the
diagnostic record that currently exists only in scattered FOIA responses,
advocacy reports, and the memories of people who went through the process and
had nowhere to put what they learned.

The person who carried documented cash and was seized anyway. The business
owner who made payroll from personal credit while waiting for a court date on
$8,000 that was eventually returned without explanation or apology. The family
whose house purchase fell through because the down payment was in an evidence
locker.

These people exist. Their records exist. The pattern they constitute is visible
to anyone who assembles it. The Diagnostic Surface gives that assembly a
durable, qualified, indestructible home.

The storm made landfall. The record holds.
