## The Terrain

Forced Arbitration is not evil.

This is the first and most important diagnostic statement in this document,
because every other analysis of forced arbitration begins with outrage, and
outrage is the least useful orientation for someone who is about to encounter
it.

Forced Arbitration is terrain. It functions as designed. It produces its
outcomes whether or not you knew it was there. It has no animus toward you. The
bear does not hate the hiker. The mountain does not want you to fall. The
clause is in the contract. You signed the contract. The process will proceed.

Understanding forced arbitration as terrain rather than as villainy produces a
more accurate diagnostic and a more useful survival posture. The bear spray
analogy is exact: a person who knows bears are in the area, knows what bear
spray is, and carries it before entering bear country has a fundamentally
different set of outcomes available to them than a person who discovers the
bears exist at the moment of encounter.

This document is the sign at the trailhead. Here is what the terrain looks like.
Here is where bears have been documented. Here is what bear spray is and where
to get it before you start hiking.

## What Forced Arbitration Is

Forced arbitration is a pre-dispute clause embedded in a contract — an
employment agreement, a credit card application, a cell phone service agreement,
a nursing home admission form, a software terms of service, a brokerage account
agreement — that requires the signing party to resolve any future dispute
through private arbitration rather than in a court of law.

It is called "forced" because:

- it is presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis with no individual
  negotiating power;
- in most markets, every competitor uses an identical clause, so declining
  means leaving the market entirely;
- it is agreed to before any dispute exists, when the consequences are abstract
  and the signing party has no reason to read carefully;
- it cannot be revoked at the moment it becomes relevant.

The clause was written by the company's attorneys to produce outcomes the
company prefers. The individual did not negotiate it. In virtually every case
they did not read it. The clause is not a mutual agreement between parties of
equal sophistication negotiating at arm's length. It is private law, written by
one party, imposed on the other as a condition of access.

## The Repeat Player Problem

The arbitrator is not a judge.

They are a private contractor, selected from a roster maintained by a private
arbitration company — the American Arbitration Association, JAMS, and others.
They are paid per case. Their professional livelihood depends on receiving cases.

The corporation that wrote the arbitration clause into its contract is a repeat
player. It appears before these arbitrators regularly. It is the source of a
significant volume of the arbitration company's revenue.

The individual consumer or employee is a one-time player. They appear once, if
ever. They have no ongoing relationship with the arbitrator or the arbitration
company.

The arbitrator who rules against the repeat player too often stops receiving
cases from that company. The arbitrator who rules for the repeat player
continues to work. This structural incentive does not require corruption in any
individual case. It does not require a phone call, a payment, or an explicit
agreement. It requires only that arbitrators are human beings who understand
where their work comes from.

This is the terrain. The slope runs in one direction.

## The Class Action Waiver: Institutional Immunity by Contract

Most modern arbitration clauses contain a class action waiver.

This is the feature that transforms arbitration from a dispute resolution
mechanism into an institutional immunity device. It deserves precise
explanation because it is the most consequential and least understood component
of the clause.

A class action allows many individual plaintiffs with similar small claims to
aggregate those claims into a single lawsuit. This is the mechanism by which a
corporation that systematically overcharges millions of customers by $30 each
can be held accountable. No individual customer can afford to litigate a $30
claim. The economics do not work. But 30 million customers aggregated represent
$900 million in potential liability, and that number attracts attorneys willing
to pursue the case on contingency.

The class action waiver eliminates this mechanism by contract, before any
dispute exists.

The result is institutional immunity from systematic small-amount misconduct.
The corporation can overcharge. Every individual customer has formally agreed,
in advance, that they will pursue any claim individually in arbitration, where
the economics of a $30 claim guarantee that no rational person will pursue it.
The right to a remedy formally exists. The practical ability to exercise it
does not. The clause was designed around this arithmetic.

## The Case Record

The following cases are public record. The decisions are final. The dissents
are written by Supreme Court Justices of the United States and describe the
structural problem with a precision this document cannot improve upon.

### AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion — 563 U.S. 333 (2011)

Vincent and Liza Concepcion signed a wireless service agreement with AT&T
Mobility in 2002. The agreement included a class action waiver in its
arbitration clause. AT&T advertised certain phones as free but charged $30.22
in sales tax on them. The Concepcions sued for false advertising and fraud.

The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that the Federal Arbitration Act preempts state
laws — including California's rule that class action waivers in consumer
contracts are unconscionable — that would invalidate such clauses.

Justice Breyer, dissenting, asked the question the majority did not answer:
what rational attorney would represent the Concepcions in litigation for the
possibility of fees from a $30.22 claim?

The answer is none. That is the point. The claim is real. The fraud, if proven,
is real. The right to remedy formally exists. The practical path to exercising
it was eliminated by the clause, and the Supreme Court said the clause must
stand.

### American Express Co. v. Italian Colors Restaurant — 570 U.S. 228 (2013)

Italian Colors Restaurant and other small merchants believed that American
Express had used its monopoly power in the charge card market to force them to
accept a tying arrangement that violated federal antitrust law — specifically
the Sherman Act. The merchants estimated that proving the antitrust claim would
cost hundreds of thousands of dollars in expert fees. The maximum individual
recovery per merchant was in the tens of thousands of dollars.

The economics made individual arbitration a mathematical impossibility. No
rational actor would spend $300,000 to recover $30,000. The merchants argued
that the class action waiver was therefore unenforceable because it eliminated
their only practical means of vindicating a federal statutory right.

The Supreme Court ruled 5-3 that the Federal Arbitration Act requires
enforcement of the waiver regardless.

Justice Kagan, dissenting, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer, stated the
situation with complete precision:

"Here is the nutshell version of this case, unfortunately obscured in the
Court's decision. The owner of a small restaurant thinks that American Express
has used its monopoly power to force merchants to accept a form contract
violating the antitrust laws. The restaurateur wants to challenge the allegedly
unlawful provision, but the same contract's arbitration clause prevents him
from doing so."

And then the direct conclusion: "Do not be fooled. Only the Court so requires;
the FAA was never meant to produce this outcome."

The dissent also noted what the majority left unstated: the ruling effectively
allowed a monopolist to use its monopoly power to insulate itself from antitrust
liability by insisting on a contract that deprived its victims of legal
recourse. The Sherman Act formally prohibited the conduct. The arbitration
clause practically immunized it.

### Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis — 584 U.S. ___ (2018)

Three consolidated cases, each involving employees who claimed their employers
had failed to pay required overtime wages. Each employee had signed a
pre-dispute arbitration agreement as a condition of employment, requiring
individual arbitration of any future employment dispute and waiving the right
to collective action.

The employees argued that the National Labor Relations Act's protection of
workers' right to engage in "concerted activities" prohibited enforcement of
the individual arbitration requirement. The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that the
Federal Arbitration Act controls and the clauses must be enforced.

By the time Epic Systems was decided, 60.1 million American workers —
56 percent of private-sector nonunion employees — were subject to forced
arbitration clauses. Of those, nearly one in three had lost the right to
challenge employer wrongdoing through collective legal action.

Justice Ginsburg, dissenting, joined by Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan,
called the decision "egregiously wrong" and a "destructive result," and stated
that "the inevitable result of today's decision will be the underenforcement of
federal and state statutes designed to advance the well-being of vulnerable
workers."

She cited documented cases: Fox News, where forced arbitration clauses
concealed decades of sexual harassment. Sterling Jewelers, parent company of
Jared and Kay Jewelers, where more than 250 women alleged rampant harassment
hidden behind mandatory individual arbitration requirements.

The dissent described the structural result with precision: individual
arbitration of employment claims involving small amounts is economically
non-viable. The claim formally exists. The practical path to exercising it has
been eliminated by the clause. The employer knows this. The clause was designed
around this knowledge.

## The Suppression of Reform: A Documented Record

The following is not analysis. It is a chronological record of what happened
when the regulatory structure attempted to address forced arbitration.

**July 10, 2017**: The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, after a three-year
congressionally mandated study of the impact of forced arbitration clauses on
consumers, issued a final rule prohibiting financial service providers from
using pre-dispute arbitration clauses that contain class action bans.

**July 25, 2017**: Fifteen days later, the House of Representatives voted to
repeal the rule under the Congressional Review Act, which permits Congress to
overturn federal regulations by simple majority vote.

**October 24, 2017**: The Senate voted 51-50 to nullify the rule. Vice
President Pence cast the deciding vote. Only two Republican senators — Lindsey
Graham and John Kennedy — voted against repeal.

**November 2017**: President Trump signed the repeal. The rule was removed from
the Code of Federal Regulations. Under the Congressional Review Act, a
substantially similar rule cannot be reissued without specific new legislative
authorization.

The CFPB study that motivated the rule had found the following: consumers who
went through individual arbitration received an average of $5,000 in recovery.
Over the five-year period studied, 34 million consumers were eligible to receive
more than $1 billion in payments through class actions. Of the tens of millions
of consumers covered by mandatory arbitration agreements, only an average of
616 individuals per year filed individual arbitration cases.

The reform was issued. The industry opposed it. The legislative mechanism
designed for fast reversal of regulations was applied. The reform was erased
in 106 days from issuance to removal.

The industry did not argue that arbitration was fair. They argued that class
actions were costly. The argument that prevailed was the institutional cost
argument. The argument that lost was the consumer access argument.

This sequence is the suppression record. It is documented in the Federal
Register, in Congressional voting records, and in the public record of the
legislative process. It is not alleged. It happened.

## Recognition: The Bear Spray

Forced arbitration clauses appear in contracts that most people sign without
reading. Recognition is the first and most available defensive posture.

A forced arbitration clause typically contains the following language or its
functional equivalent:

- "Any dispute arising out of or relating to this agreement shall be resolved
  by binding arbitration..."
- "You waive your right to a jury trial..."
- "Claims may not be brought as a class action, class arbitration, or in any
  representative capacity..."
- "The arbitrator's decision shall be final and binding..."

These phrases, in any combination, are the diagnostic signal. The clause is in
the contract. It is usually in a section labeled "Dispute Resolution" or
"Arbitration Agreement" or buried in a general terms and conditions section.

It appears in:

- Employment offer letters and employee handbooks
- Cell phone, cable, and internet service agreements
- Credit card cardholder agreements
- Bank account terms
- Brokerage and investment account agreements
- Nursing home and elder care admission documents
- Software and platform terms of service
- Ride-sharing and gig economy contractor agreements
- Gym memberships
- Student loan servicer agreements

The bear spray is recognition at the moment of signing, before the dispute
exists, when alternatives may still be available. Negotiation of employment
arbitration clauses is possible in some contexts, particularly for professional
and executive roles. Choosing providers who do not use such clauses, where
competition permits, is possible in some markets. Documenting the clause at the
time of signing — its specific language, the date, the version of the agreement
— preserves the evidentiary record for any future diagnostic surface.

After the dispute exists, the clause is almost always enforceable. The terrain
does not change once you are on it.

## What Civic Infrastructure Does With This

The Diagnostic Surface for Forced Arbitration cannot change the outcome of an
arbitration that has already occurred under an enforced clause.

What it can do is what the infrastructure is designed to do: make the pattern
legible, durable, and indestructible — before, during, and after the
institutional narrative is written.

The arbitration clause that produced a pre-determined outcome is a document. The
arbitration proceeding record is a document. The arbitrator's award is a
document. The comparison between outcomes in arbitration and outcomes in
comparable litigation is a documented record across thousands of cases.

None of these documents, individually, are sufficient. Assembled, qualified by
affected-status standing, evidence-disciplined, and pinned to a
content-addressed storage system that no institutional actor can suppress, they
constitute the diagnostic record that the affected person currently has nowhere
to put.

The three Supreme Court dissents cited in this document — Breyer in
*Concepcion*, Kagan in *Italian Colors*, Ginsburg in *Epic Systems* — are
themselves diagnostic records written by the most credentialed possible authors.
They described the structural problem with complete precision. They lost. The
decisions they dissented from are final law.

But the dissents are indestructible. They are in the public record. They cannot
be repealed by the Congressional Review Act. They cannot be taken down by a
legal threat to a web host. They are cited here, in a document that is itself
published to a content-addressed system, for exactly that reason.

The record holds.
